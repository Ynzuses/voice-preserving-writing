# Results and successive revisions

Read the iteration section for feedback rounds or marked passages. Read the results section for prose that explains methods, numerical evidence and conclusions. These are editing decisions, not a required report template.

## Iteration: diagnose before replacing

Use the latest authorized draft as the baseline. The user's approval of some passages is evidence that they work; preserve them unless an accuracy problem or an affected dependency requires repair. A marked passage is a review location, not a diagnosis.

Separate the scope of revision from the source of feedback. A request to rebuild permits reconsidering the argument and, where the user leaves it open, the illustrative case; use the short-answer reference for that work. Approval of the main approach followed by local feedback calls for a local pass. Unmarked text is not automatically correct, and marked text is not automatically weak.

Name the actual problem before choosing a change: an unexplained decision, detached metric, unclear referent, missing comparison, repetitive conclusion, inflated claim, or wording that conflicts with demonstrated voice. When no such problem is present, keep accurate technical reporting even if an automated tool flags it.

For each affected passage, protect the facts, numbers, units, denominators, evaluation setting, citations and uncertainty. Change the relationship or reader order before trying synonyms. Typical useful repairs are:

- connect a method choice to the actual constraint instead of saying it is simply suitable;
- bring the evidence and its practical meaning together instead of postponing every interpretation to the final paragraph;
- replace a broad quality label with the specific capability or error that warrants it;
- remove a repeated explanation, or clarify which result a limitation qualifies.

Check other passages only where the same defect occurs. A local edit should not trigger an automatic rewrite of the opening, all transitions or every sentence length. Natural variation follows what each sentence needs to do; do not insert errors, anecdotes, casual language or filler to manufacture a voice.

Compare the candidate with the baseline on prompt coverage, factual fidelity, traceable reasoning, ease of reading, voice and length. A lower detector score cannot compensate for a lost requirement or weakened explanation. If another pass adds no identifiable benefit, stop or restore the stronger wording. Later does not automatically mean better.

Before returning a local revision, compare it with the adopted baseline and inspect every change outside the requested span. Keep accepted paragraphs verbatim when their content and dependencies are unaffected. A one-sentence request does not authorize refreshing the introduction, changing the case or shortening the rest. If an accuracy correction needs a wider edit, explain the dependency briefly.

Separate editorial acceptance, an automated observation, and delivery status. Preserve the exact chosen text when the user confirms submission; a copy-ready request or a favorable score does not establish that submission happened. Return the full combined text when requested, integrating only the adopted edits. Follow host rules for any surrounding status report and keep it outside the copyable body.

When editing files, retain the previous candidate and identify which version is current under the workspace's existing conventions. Record automated feedback separately from editorial acceptance when such a record is needed. Do not treat a request for another edit as finalization, or overwrite a submitted baseline. A rollback should be possible without reconstructing text from chat.

## Results: let the reader assess the judgment

Start from what the prompt asks the reader to understand. In a short applied report, this may be the method and its rationale, how it was evaluated, what it found, and the limits of that finding. Use the sequence the task needs; do not impose this order on every genre.

Explain choices using available facts. For example, if a draft already identifies sparse observations and a regularization penalty, explain how the penalty constrains the fit. Do not invent a model comparison, an experiment, or a personal motivation merely to make the account sound specific. First-person process claims must agree with the supplied work record.

Keep evaluation contexts distinguishable. Results on data used for fitting, internal validation, and an untouched test set answer different questions. Name which one a figure belongs to. Pooled held-out predictions and a mean of fold scores are different summaries; preserve the summary actually computed. Do not turn a score difference into proof of overfitting or stable generalization without adequate support.

Use counts or denominators when they resolve a reading question. Precision is easier to interpret when the reader knows how many positive predictions were correct; sensitivity addresses how many actual cases were found. This does not require spelling out every fraction or repeating the entire confusion matrix. A technical audience may be better served by a compact table followed by the interpretation.

Keep the numerical account and its meaning adjacent where helpful. A sentence such as "The model performed well" needs a criterion. Explain which outcome it achieved, what errors remained and why those errors matter for the stated task. High accuracy alone may be a poor criterion when a common class dominates; use a relevant baseline if it was supplied or can be derived reliably. Do not infer a ranking metric or calibration from a confusion matrix.

Within a tight word limit, retain the metrics the prompt requires and the evidence needed for the main judgment. Remove duplicate calculations and optional secondary detail before cutting workflow, model rationale or interpretation. Put fuller evidence in an existing table or analysis record when appropriate; do not create an appendix merely to avoid answering within the limit.

Make limitations specific to the evidence: few observed events, one setting, an untested population, a validation design, or an unexamined probability estimate. Explain what remains unknown without converting a sensible future check into an experiment already completed. Source descriptions should support scope; avoid a long provenance detour when a brief phrase and citation suffice. Do not claim clinical suitability, external validity or calibrated risk from discrimination alone.
